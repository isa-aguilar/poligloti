# English syllabus (explanations in English)

## A1

### a1-01 · The verb to be and introductions
- **Grammar**: present of the verb *to be* (I am, you are, he/she/it is, we/you/they are), its contractions (I'm, you're, she's) and the negative (isn't, aren't) and question forms (Are you...?, Is he...?).
- **Goal**: introduce yourself, give personal details and greet people.
- **Key points**:
  - I'm + name; I'm from + country; I'm a + job
  - questions: What's your name? Where are you from?
  - greetings: Hello, Hi, Good morning, Nice to meet you, See you
- **Examples**:
  - Hi, I'm Lena. I'm from Portugal and I work in a design agency.
  - This is my colleague, Marc. He's the project manager.
  - Nice to meet you. How are you?
- **Suggested practice**: introduce yourself to a new colleague in a meeting; introduce another person from the team.

### a1-02 · Present simple and routines
- **Grammar**: present simple for habits and routines. The third person singular adds -s/-es (he works, she goes). Negatives and questions use the auxiliary do/does (I don't work, Does she work?).
- **Goal**: talk about routines, schedules and habitual facts.
- **Key points**:
  - I work / he works; I go / she goes (third person with -s)
  - negative: I don't start before nine; question: Do you work here?
  - adverbs of frequency: always, usually, often, sometimes, never
- **Examples**:
  - I usually start work at nine and check my emails first.
  - She works in the finance team.
  - Do you have meetings on Mondays?
- **Suggested practice**: describe your typical working day; ask a colleague about their routine.

### a1-03 · Articles a / an / the
- **Grammar**: indefinite article *a* (before a consonant sound: a report, a university) / *an* (before a vowel sound: an email, an hour) for something non-specific or mentioned for the first time: the sound decides, not the letter. Definite article *the* for something specific or already known. No article with plurals and uncountable nouns used in a general sense.
- **Goal**: use articles correctly when naming things.
- **Key points**:
  - a report, an email, an hour (an because of the sound, not the letter)
  - the with something specific: the report I sent you
  - no article in general statements: Meetings are useful.
- **Examples**:
  - I need to send an email and book a meeting room.
  - The client from Germany is arriving today.
  - It takes an hour to get to the airport.
- **Suggested practice**: describe three office objects with a/an; talk about something specific with the.

### a1-04 · There is / there are and prepositions of place
- **Grammar**: *there is* (singular) / *there are* (plural) to express existence. Prepositions of place: in, on, at, under, next to, between, in front of, behind.
- **Goal**: describe places and say where things are.
- **Key points**:
  - There is a problem / There are two options.
  - negative: There isn't any time; question: Is there a lift?
  - at for points, in for enclosed spaces, on for surfaces
- **Examples**:
  - There's a coffee machine next to the meeting room.
  - Are there any seats left on the flight?
  - Your suitcase is behind the counter.
- **Suggested practice**: describe what there is in your office; ask at a hotel whether they have something with Is there...?

### a1-05 · Can / can't and requests
- **Grammar**: the modal *can* for ability, possibility and permission; its negative *can't*. Requests and offers with Can/Could you...?, Can I...? The main verb goes in the bare infinitive (without to).
- **Goal**: express ability and make polite requests.
- **Key points**:
  - I can speak some German. / I can't come tomorrow.
  - request: Can you send me the file? / Could you repeat that? (more polite)
  - permission: Can I ask a question?
- **Examples**:
  - Can you help me with the presentation?
  - I can't join the call at three, sorry.
  - Could I have the bill, please?
- **Suggested practice**: ask a colleague for a favour with Can you...?; order something in a restaurant with Could I...?

### a1-06 · Present continuous
- **Grammar**: present continuous (am/is/are + verb-ing) for actions in progress now or for concrete near-future plans. Contrast with the present simple (routine versus what is happening now).
- **Goal**: describe what is happening and immediate plans.
- **Key points**:
  - I'm working on the report right now.
  - -ing spelling rules: make/making, sit/sitting, run/running
  - near-future plans: We're meeting the client tomorrow.
- **Examples**:
  - I'm preparing the slides for the meeting.
  - They're waiting for us at the airport.
  - What are you working on this week?
- **Suggested practice**: say what you are doing right now; describe what is happening in the office.

### a1-07 · Past simple
- **Grammar**: past simple for finished actions. Regular verbs add -ed (worked, called); basic irregular verbs have to be memorised (went, had, saw, made, said, took). Negatives and questions use didn't / did (with the verb in the infinitive).
- **Goal**: talk about past events and experiences.
- **Key points**:
  - regular: I worked, I called; irregular: I went, I had, I saw
  - negative: I didn't finish; question: Did you send it?
  - time markers: yesterday, last week, two days ago
- **Examples**:
  - I sent the report yesterday and called the client.
  - We had a long meeting last Monday.
  - Did you talk to the supplier?
- **Suggested practice**: say what you did at work yesterday; ask a colleague about something they did last week.

### a1-08 · Countable and uncountable nouns, some / any
- **Grammar**: countable nouns (a report, two reports) and uncountable nouns (information, time, work, money). Quantifiers: *some* in affirmative sentences, *any* in negatives and questions; much (uncountable), many (countable), a lot of (both).
- **Goal**: talk about indefinite quantities.
- **Key points**:
  - some emails, any questions; how much time, how many people
  - common uncountable nouns: information, advice, feedback, work
  - a lot of works with both countable and uncountable nouns
- **Examples**:
  - Do you have any questions about the project?
  - I need some information before the meeting.
  - We don't have much time before the deadline.
- **Suggested practice**: ask whether there is something with any; say what you need at work with some.

### a1-09 · Comparatives
- **Grammar**: comparative adjectives: short ones add -er ... than (faster, cheaper; with a doubled consonant in big/bigger); two-syllable adjectives ending in -y change -y to -ier (easy/easier, happy/happier, busy/busier); long ones use more ... than (more expensive, more efficient). Equality with as ... as. Irregular forms: good/better, bad/worse, far/further.
- **Goal**: compare options, people and things.
- **Key points**:
  - The train is faster than the car.
  - more + long adjective: more efficient, more important
  - as cheap as, not as fast as (equality and inequality)
- **Examples**:
  - The train is cheaper than the plane.
  - This option is more efficient than the current one.
  - Today's meeting was better than last week's.
- **Suggested practice**: compare two ways of travelling for a trip; compare two work proposals.

### a1-10 · Going to and plans
- **Grammar**: future with *be going to* + infinitive for intentions and decided plans, and for predictions based on evidence. Brief contrast with will (decisions made in the moment and general predictions).
- **Goal**: talk about plans and intentions.
- **Key points**:
  - I'm going to send the report this afternoon.
  - negative/question: I'm not going to..., Are you going to...?
  - prediction from evidence: It's going to rain.
- **Examples**:
  - We're going to launch the project next month.
  - I'm going to travel to Berlin for the conference.
  - Are you going to join the dinner tonight?
- **Suggested practice**: describe your plans for the week with going to; ask a colleague about their plans.

## A2

### a2-01 · Past simple in depth
- **Grammar**: consolidating the past simple with more irregular verbs and wh- questions (What did you do?, Where did you go?). Review of the negative/question structure with did.
- **Goal**: narrate the past fluently and in detail.
- **Key points**:
  - common irregular verbs: bought, brought, thought, found, met, left
  - wh- questions: When did the meeting start? Who did you meet?
  - in questions and negatives the verb goes back to the infinitive
- **Examples**:
  - I met the new supplier and we discussed the contract.
  - Where did you go on your last trip?
  - The flight left late, so I missed the connection.
- **Suggested practice**: describe a past working day in detail; interview a colleague about a trip.

### a2-02 · Introduction to the present perfect (ever / never / just)
- **Grammar**: present perfect (have/has + past participle) for experiences with no specific time and recent actions with a present result. Markers: ever/never (experience), just/already in affirmative sentences, yet in negatives and questions. The key contrast with the past simple: the present perfect never takes a finished-time marker (no yesterday, last week, in 2020); those need the past simple.
- **Goal**: talk about experiences and recent actions.
- **Key points**:
  - Have you ever been to Germany? / I've never tried it.
  - just, already, yet: I've just sent it. Have you finished yet?
  - participles: been, done, seen, made, written
- **Examples**:
  - I've just finished the report.
  - Have you ever worked with this client before?
  - She hasn't replied to the email yet.
- **Suggested practice**: ask about experiences with Have you ever...?; say what you have just done with just.

### a2-03 · Will vs going to
- **Grammar**: the difference between *will* (spontaneous decisions, offers, promises, predictions without evidence) and *be going to* (prior plans and predictions based on evidence). Will + bare infinitive.
- **Goal**: choose the right future form for the intention.
- **Key points**:
  - decision in the moment: I'll call him now.
  - prior plan: I'm going to call him this afternoon.
  - promise/offer: I'll help you. / I'll send it today.
- **Examples**:
  - Don't worry, I'll take care of the booking.
  - We're going to hire two more people next quarter.
  - I think the project will succeed.
- **Suggested practice**: offer to do something with I'll; describe a plan you have already decided with going to.

### a2-04 · Have to / must
- **Grammar**: obligation with *have to* (external, from circumstances) and *must* (strong, from the speaker). Lack of obligation with *don't have to* (it isn't necessary), as opposed to *mustn't* (prohibition). Should for advice.
- **Goal**: express obligation, prohibition and lack of obligation.
- **Key points**:
  - I have to finish this by Friday. (external obligation)
  - You mustn't share this document. (prohibition)
  - You don't have to come, it's optional. (not necessary)
- **Examples**:
  - We have to submit the numbers before the meeting.
  - You don't have to stay late today.
  - Passengers must show their boarding pass.
- **Suggested practice**: explain your obligations this week with have to; contrast don't have to and mustn't with two examples.

### a2-05 · Adverbs of frequency and manner
- **Grammar**: adverbs of frequency (always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, never) and their position (before the main verb, after to be). Adverbs of manner (formed with -ly: quickly, carefully) describe how something is done.
- **Goal**: say how often and in what way actions happen.
- **Key points**:
  - I always check my emails first. (before the verb)
  - She is often late. (after to be)
  - manner: He speaks clearly. She works efficiently.
- **Examples**:
  - I usually reply to emails in the morning.
  - Please read the contract carefully.
  - We rarely have problems with this supplier.
- **Suggested practice**: describe how often you do three work tasks; describe how to do a task well with an adverb of manner.

### a2-06 · Forming questions
- **Grammar**: systematic review of question formation: yes/no questions (auxiliary + subject), wh- questions (What, Where, When, Why, Who, How), subject questions without an auxiliary (Who called?) and question words with a preposition (What ... about?).
- **Goal**: ask questions correctly in different tenses.
- **Key points**:
  - Where do you work? When did the meeting start?
  - subject question: Who sent this email? (no do)
  - How long, How often, How much, What kind of
- **Examples**:
  - How often do you travel for work?
  - Who is responsible for the delivery?
  - What are we going to talk about?
- **Suggested practice**: prepare five questions for a project meeting; interview a colleague with wh- questions.

### a2-07 · Used to
- **Grammar**: *used to* + infinitive for past habits and states that no longer happen. Negative and question forms with didn't use to / Did you use to...?
- **Goal**: contrast how things were before with how they are now.
- **Key points**:
  - I used to work in another department.
  - negative: We didn't use to have remote meetings.
  - question: Did you use to travel a lot?
- **Examples**:
  - I used to commute every day, but now I work from home.
  - We used to use a different system for bookings.
  - Did you use to work in sales?
- **Suggested practice**: contrast your old job with your current one using used to; describe a habit you have lost.

### a2-08 · First conditional
- **Grammar**: first conditional for real or likely future situations: If + present simple, will + infinitive. Careful: the if-clause takes the present, never will (If we finish today..., not If we will finish...). Use a comma when the if-clause comes first. Alternative: unless (= if not).
- **Goal**: talk about the likely consequences of future conditions.
- **Key points**:
  - If we finish today, we'll have time to review.
  - either order: We'll have time to review if we finish today.
  - unless = if not: Unless it rains, we'll walk.
- **Examples**:
  - If the client agrees, we'll sign the contract this week.
  - If you send it now, I'll check it before lunch.
  - We'll miss the flight unless we leave now.
- **Suggested practice**: set out a work condition and its consequence; use unless to give a warning.

### a2-09 · Too / enough
- **Grammar**: *too* + adjective (excess, negative meaning) and *enough* (sufficient): enough goes before nouns but after adjectives/adverbs (enough time, big enough). too much/too many with nouns.
- **Goal**: express excess and sufficiency.
- **Key points**:
  - The room is too small for the team.
  - We don't have enough time. / The room isn't big enough.
  - too much work, too many meetings
- **Examples**:
  - This deadline is too tight.
  - We don't have enough people for the project.
  - There are too many meetings this week.
- **Suggested practice**: politely complain about something excessive at work with too; say what is missing with enough.

### a2-10 · Basic phrasal verbs
- **Grammar**: common phrasal verbs (verb + particle): set up, find out, deal with, look for, fill in, follow up, carry out. Some are separable (fill it in), others are not (deal with it).
- **Goal**: use common phrasal verbs from work and daily life.
- **Key points**:
  - set up a meeting, find out the details, deal with a problem
  - separable: fill in the form / fill the form in / fill it in
  - inseparable: look for it, deal with it (not "look it for")
- **Examples**:
  - Can you set up a call with the supplier? (set up = organise)
  - I'll find out what happened with the delivery. (find out = discover)
  - Please fill in the form and send it back. (fill in = complete)
- **Suggested practice**: describe three work tasks using phrasal verbs; ask a colleague to organise something with set up.

### a2-11 · Relative clauses (who / which / that)
- **Grammar**: defining relative clauses with *who* (people), *which* (things) and *that* (both). The relative pronoun can be left out when it is the object (the report (that) I sent). *where* and *whose* for place and possession.
- **Goal**: give information about people and things in a single sentence.
- **Key points**:
  - The colleague who leads the team...
  - The report which/that we sent...
  - the office where I work; the client whose contract expired
- **Examples**:
  - She's the manager who approved the budget.
  - This is the tool that we use for tracking orders.
  - That's the hotel where we stayed last time.
- **Suggested practice**: describe a colleague with who; describe a tool or process with which/that.

### a2-12 · State verbs
- **Grammar**: state verbs (know, want, like, need, believe, understand, own, mean) are not normally used in the continuous form because they describe states, not actions. Some change meaning (I think = my opinion is / I'm thinking = I'm considering).
- **Goal**: use the present simple correctly with state verbs.
- **Key points**:
  - I know the answer. (not "I'm knowing")
  - I want / I need / I understand in the present simple
  - two uses: I think it's fine (opinion) vs I'm thinking about it (process)
- **Examples**:
  - I understand the problem, but I need more time.
  - She wants to join the project.
  - I think this plan makes sense.
- **Suggested practice**: give your opinion on a plan with think/believe; express needs and wishes with want/need.

## B1

### b1-01 · Present perfect vs past simple (for / since)
- **Grammar**: contrast between the present perfect (unfinished time or present relevance) and the past simple (a finished, specific time). Present perfect with *for* + period (for two years), *since* + starting point (since 2020) and adverbs such as already, yet, just, ever, never, so far; past simple with finished-time markers (yesterday, in 2019, two days ago). Present perfect continuous for ongoing actions (I've been working).
- **Goal**: talk about career paths, duration and experiences precisely.
- **Key points**:
  - I've worked here for five years. (still true) vs I worked there in 2019. (finished)
  - for + duration, since + starting point
  - I've been waiting for an hour. (present perfect continuous)
- **Examples**:
  - I've worked in healthcare since 2018.
  - We've had this supplier for three years.
  - I've been preparing the presentation all morning.
- **Suggested practice**: describe your career with for/since; distinguish a finished experience from one that is still ongoing.

### b1-02 · Past continuous vs past simple
- **Grammar**: past continuous (was/were + -ing) for actions in progress in the past, often interrupted by an action in the past simple. Linkers: when (single action), while (action in progress).
- **Goal**: narrate with background actions and single events.
- **Key points**:
  - I was working when the client called.
  - While we were meeting, the system went down.
  - two simultaneous background actions: While I was writing, she was calling.
- **Examples**:
  - I was preparing the report when the meeting got cancelled.
  - While we were flying to Berlin, they sent the new numbers.
  - What were you doing when the email arrived?
- **Suggested practice**: say what you were doing when something happened at work; tell a travel anecdote with when/while.

### b1-03 · Second conditional
- **Grammar**: second conditional for hypothetical or unlikely situations in the present/future: If + past simple, would + infinitive. Advice with If I were you, I would... (were for all persons).
- **Goal**: talk about hypotheses and give advice.
- **Key points**:
  - If we had more budget, we would hire more people.
  - If I were you, I'd talk to the manager. (advice)
  - would + infinitive in the main clause
- **Examples**:
  - If I had more time, I would redo the whole presentation.
  - If the deadline were later, we wouldn't be so stressed.
  - If I were you, I would raise this in the meeting.
- **Suggested practice**: give a colleague advice with If I were you; set out a hypothesis about work with the second conditional.

### b1-04 · Present and past passive
- **Grammar**: passive voice with be + past participle to focus on the action rather than the agent. Present (is done), past (was done), present perfect (has been done), future (will be done). The agent, if mentioned, goes with by.
- **Goal**: describe processes and results in an impersonal, formal way.
- **Key points**:
  - The report is reviewed every month. (present passive)
  - The contract was signed yesterday. (past passive)
  - The order has been shipped. (present perfect passive)
- **Examples**:
  - The invoices are sent at the end of the month.
  - The decision was made by the board.
  - Your request has been processed.
- **Suggested practice**: describe a process at your work in the passive; say what was decided or done using the past passive.

### b1-05 · Basic reported speech
- **Grammar**: reported speech: the tense usually moves one step back (present -> past, will -> would, can -> could; past simple and present perfect -> past perfect) and pronouns and time markers change. Reporting verbs: say, tell, ask. Reported questions use statement word order, with no inversion and no do: yes/no questions with if/whether (asked if I would come) and wh- questions keeping the question word (asked where the office was).
- **Goal**: report what other people said and asked.
- **Key points**:
  - "I'm busy" -> He said (that) he was busy.
  - "Will you come?" -> She asked if I would come.
  - tell + person: She told me that...
- **Examples**:
  - The client said the delivery was late.
  - She asked if I had finished the report.
  - He told me he would call back later.
- **Suggested practice**: report three things someone said in a meeting; pass on a client's question in reported speech.

### b1-06 · Modals of deduction (must / might / can't)
- **Grammar**: modals expressing degrees of certainty about the present: *must* (near-certain deduction), *might/may/could* (possibility), *can't* (deduced impossibility). Main verb in the bare infinitive.
- **Goal**: express hypotheses and deductions with different degrees of certainty.
- **Key points**:
  - She must be in a meeting. (confident deduction)
  - It might be a problem with the system. (possibility)
  - That can't be right. (impossible from what I know)
- **Examples**:
  - The client hasn't replied; he must be travelling.
  - The delay might be due to customs.
  - This figure can't be correct.
- **Suggested practice**: deduce why a colleague isn't replying with must/might/can't; speculate about the cause of a problem.

### b1-07 · Gerund vs infinitive
- **Grammar**: choosing between the gerund (-ing) and the infinitive (to + verb) after other verbs. Gerund after enjoy, avoid, suggest, finish, and after prepositions. Infinitive after want, need, decide, hope, agree. Some verbs change meaning (remember/stop).
- **Goal**: combine verbs correctly.
- **Key points**:
  - I enjoy working with the team. / I want to finish early.
  - after a preposition always -ing: interested in learning
  - stop doing (quit) vs stop to do (pause in order to)
- **Examples**:
  - We decided to postpone the launch.
  - I'm interested in joining the new project.
  - She suggested changing the supplier.
- **Suggested practice**: say what you enjoy and what you avoid at work (gerund); say what you have decided or want to do (infinitive).

### b1-08 · Non-defining and defining relative clauses
- **Grammar**: the difference between defining relative clauses (they identify, no commas, that is possible) and non-defining ones (they add extra information, between commas, no that, and the pronoun cannot be left out). Using which to comment on the whole sentence.
- **Goal**: give essential or additional information precisely.
- **Key points**:
  - defining: The report that I sent is final. (identifies which one)
  - non-defining: The report, which I sent yesterday, is final. (extra info)
  - which commenting on the sentence: They cancelled it, which was annoying.
- **Examples**:
  - Our new manager, who joined last month, is from Munich.
  - The flight, which was delayed by two hours, finally left.
  - The tool that we use is quite reliable.
- **Suggested practice**: describe a colleague with a non-defining clause between commas; identify a specific project with a defining clause.

### b1-09 · Advanced quantifiers
- **Grammar**: finer quantifiers: few / a few (countable), little / a little (uncountable), several, plenty of, most (of), none of, both, neither, either. The difference between few (not many, negative) and a few (some, positive).
- **Goal**: express quantities with precision.
- **Key points**:
  - a few options (some) vs few options (not enough)
  - a little time vs little time
  - most of the team, none of the clients, both proposals
- **Examples**:
  - We have a few good candidates for the role.
  - There's little time left before the deadline.
  - Most of the team agreed with the plan.
- **Suggested practice**: describe a project's resources with few/a few and little/a little; summarise a team vote with most/none/both.

### b1-10 · Question tags
- **Grammar**: question tags: affirmative sentence + negative tag, negative sentence + affirmative tag, repeating the auxiliary (You're coming, aren't you? He didn't call, did he?). They are used to confirm something or to seek agreement.
- **Goal**: confirm information and sound more natural in conversation.
- **Key points**:
  - affirmative -> negative tag: You sent it, didn't you?
  - negative -> affirmative tag: She isn't here, is she?
  - with I am -> aren't I?
- **Examples**:
  - The meeting is at three, isn't it?
  - You didn't forget the report, did you?
  - We've met before, haven't we?
- **Suggested practice**: confirm meeting details with question tags; seek a colleague's agreement with a tag.

### b1-11 · Introduction to the third conditional
- **Grammar**: third conditional for unreal past situations and consequences that did not happen: If + past perfect, would have + past participle. It expresses regret or hypotheses about what did not happen.
- **Goal**: talk about hypothetical past situations and their consequences.
- **Key points**:
  - If we had started earlier, we would have finished on time.
  - would have + past participle in the main clause
  - often expresses regret or mild criticism
- **Examples**:
  - If I had known, I would have told you.
  - If we had booked earlier, the flight would have been cheaper.
  - The meeting would have gone better if we had prepared more.
- **Suggested practice**: say what you would have done differently on a project with the third conditional; analyse a past mistake without blaming anyone.

### b1-12 · Linkers (although / despite / however)
- **Grammar**: contrast linkers: *although / even though* + clause; *despite / in spite of* + noun or -ing; *however* as an adverb between sentences (with a full stop or semicolon). Contrast with because/so for cause and consequence.
- **Goal**: express contrast and concession in a careful register.
- **Key points**:
  - Although it was late, we finished the report.
  - Despite the delay, the client was happy. (despite + noun)
  - It was risky. However, we decided to go ahead.
- **Examples**:
  - Although the budget is tight, we can deliver on time.
  - Despite the technical problems, the launch went well.
  - The numbers are good. However, we should stay cautious.
- **Suggested practice**: qualify a piece of good news with however; express concession about a project with although/despite.

## B2

### b2-01 · Narrative tenses
- **Grammar**: combining tenses to narrate: past simple (main action), past continuous (background action), past perfect (an action before another past action) and past perfect continuous (duration up to a past point). Essential for stories and incident reports.
- **Goal**: narrate complex events in a clear time order.
- **Key points**:
  - past perfect for what came earlier: We had already left when they called.
  - combination: I was reviewing the report I had written when the client arrived.
  - past perfect continuous: She had been working on it for hours before the crash.
- **Examples**:
  - By the time the meeting started, we had prepared everything.
  - The system crashed while I was uploading the files I had just finished.
  - They had been negotiating for weeks before they signed.
- **Suggested practice**: narrate a work incident combining all four tenses; tell a travel anecdote in a clear time order.

### b2-02 · Mixed conditionals
- **Grammar**: mixed conditionals combine tenses: a past condition with a present consequence (If I had studied German, I would understand this now) or a present condition with a past consequence (If I were more organised, I wouldn't have missed it).
- **Goal**: link causes and consequences across past and present.
- **Key points**:
  - past -> present: If we had hired her, she would be leading the team now.
  - present -> past: If I were better at planning, I wouldn't have forgotten.
  - mixing past perfect / would + infinitive or past simple / would have
- **Examples**:
  - If I had taken that job, I would be working in Berlin now.
  - If she were more experienced, she wouldn't have made that mistake.
  - If we had invested earlier, we would have a bigger team today.
- **Suggested practice**: link a past decision to your current situation; analyse how one of your traits influenced something in the past.

### b2-03 · Wish / if only
- **Grammar**: *wish / if only* for wishes and regrets: wish + past simple (unreal present wish), wish + past perfect (regret about the past), wish + would (complaint about someone else's behaviour).
- **Goal**: express wishes, regrets and complaints.
- **Key points**:
  - I wish I had more time. (present wish)
  - I wish I had prepared better. (past regret)
  - I wish they would reply faster. (complaint about others)
- **Examples**:
  - I wish I spoke German fluently.
  - If only we had booked the earlier flight.
  - I wish the client would make a decision.
- **Suggested practice**: express three wishes about your work or your languages; politely complain about someone's behaviour with wish + would.

### b2-04 · Causative have / get
- **Grammar**: causative structure have/get + something + past participle to say that someone else does something for you (I had the report translated). Contrast with the active form (I translated it myself).
- **Goal**: talk about services and tasks done by other people.
- **Key points**:
  - I had the documents translated. (I arranged for someone to translate them)
  - We're getting the office repainted. (with get, more informal)
  - contrast: I fixed it (I did it) vs I had it fixed (someone did it for me)
- **Examples**:
  - We had the contract reviewed by legal.
  - I need to get my laptop repaired before the trip.
  - They had the numbers double-checked before the meeting.
- **Suggested practice**: describe three things you had others do at work with have something done; talk about a service with get.

### b2-05 · Basic inversion (not only, hardly)
- **Grammar**: inversion of subject and auxiliary after negative or restrictive expressions at the start of the sentence, for formal emphasis: Not only did we..., Hardly had I..., Never have I..., No sooner... than.
- **Goal**: add emphasis in formal written and spoken register.
- **Key points**:
  - Not only did we finish, but we also saved money.
  - Hardly had the meeting started when the system failed.
  - Never have I seen such a tight deadline.
- **Examples**:
  - Not only did we meet the deadline, but we also exceeded the target.
  - Never have we had such a smooth launch.
  - No sooner had I sent the email than the client called.
- **Suggested practice**: highlight a team achievement with Not only ... but also; start a formal sentence with Never have I / we.

### b2-06 · Advanced passives (be said to, be supposed to)
- **Grammar**: impersonal passive constructions for reporting or attributing information, with two equivalent patterns: impersonal with it (It is said/believed/expected that...) and personal with an infinitive (He is said to..., is believed to..., is expected to...). Typical verbs: say, believe, expect, report, think, consider. Careful with be supposed to: it is usually NOT this reporting passive but a semi-modal of expectation or obligation, often unfulfilled (We were supposed to launch = that was the plan, and it didn't happen); only when it means "to have a reputation for" (It's supposed to be good) is it equivalent to is said to.
- **Goal**: report information impersonally and qualify the source.
- **Key points**:
  - It is said that the market is recovering.
  - The delivery is supposed to arrive today. (that is the expectation)
  - The new tool is expected to save time.
- **Examples**:
  - The report is expected to be ready by Friday.
  - The client is said to be very demanding.
  - We were supposed to launch last month. (but we didn't)
- **Suggested practice**: report expectations about a project with be expected to / be supposed to; attribute an opinion without naming the source.

### b2-07 · Advanced relative clauses and participle clauses
- **Grammar**: relative clauses with a preposition in formal register (the client to whom I spoke, the project on which we worked) and quantifier + relative pronoun (all of which, many of whom). Participle clauses to condense information (Having finished the report, I left; the report written last week). In adverbial participle clauses, the implied subject of the participle must be the same as the subject of the main clause (Having finished the report, I left = I finished and I left); if it isn't, the sentence is badly formed (dangling participle).
- **Goal**: write and speak with condensed, formal structures.
- **Key points**:
  - formal: the supplier with whom we negotiated
  - all of which / many of whom: We had ten offers, most of which were rejected.
  - participle: Having reviewed the data, we decided to proceed.
- **Examples**:
  - We received several proposals, none of which met the budget.
  - Having analysed the results, the team changed the strategy.
  - The email, sent late on Friday, was easy to miss.
- **Suggested practice**: combine two sentences into one with a participle clause; describe options with all/none/most of which.

### b2-08 · Professional hedging and softening
- **Grammar**: hedging and polite professional language for meetings: I was wondering if..., it might be worth..., perhaps we could..., I'm not entirely sure, that said, to a certain extent. It softens proposals, disagreement and criticism.
- **Goal**: propose, disagree and qualify diplomatically at work.
- **Key points**:
  - soft proposal: I was wondering if we could push the deadline.
  - suggestion: It might be worth reviewing the budget first.
  - polite disagreement: I see your point, but I'm not entirely convinced.
- **Examples**:
  - I was wondering if we could revisit this next week.
  - It might be worth checking with the client before we commit.
  - That said, I think there's some risk we should discuss.
- **Suggested practice**: propose a deadline change in a meeting using hedging; politely disagree with an idea from your manager.
